EVAgent · day-ahead charging at a shared site

Can a language model act as a charging operator a site can trust? The design asks that of the 6 ways of answering the same requests listed below, from a deterministic parser to the solver-grounded agent, on the same 20 days of real Caltech sessions, with the same scoring. They are the same 6 rows as the power-flow case study, with the same names, so the two tables can be read side by side. 3 of the 6 run today; the rest are what the revision still has to build.

The problem

A site operator describes a day of charging in plain English: the cars, when each one arrives and leaves, how much energy it needs, what its charger can deliver. Somebody has to turn that sentence into the parameters of an optimisation problem, solve it under a 50 kW site cap and a time-of-use tariff, and answer what was actually asked. Today that somebody is an engineer with CVXPY. The question is what happens when it is a language model.

"44 cars are charging at the Caltech campus lot on 2019-05-01, and here is what each one needs. EV 1: arrives quarter past nine at night, leaves midnight at the end of the day, 1.46 kilowatt-hours, 7000 watts maximum. EV 2 needs 51850 Wh and is parked from 15:45 until half eleven at night on a 7 kilowatts charger. […] How much of the requested energy cannot be delivered?"

One of 10 request variants per day. Times are spelled in words, energies come in Wh or kWh, power in W or kW, and the sentence shapes vary, because reading the request is half of the task being measured.

What a correct answer needs

  • Formulation: every session's arrival, departure, energy and max power, read from the text.
  • A runnable schedule: inside every window, under every charger limit, under the site cap at every step.
  • The operator's answer: the cost, the undelivered energy, the share served, the car — and it must describe the schedule that was actually produced.

What can go wrong

  • A schedule that looks fine and breaks the site cap: wrong, presented as right.
  • A cheap schedule that is cheap because it never charges the cars.
  • An answer quoting one solve while the schedule on the table is another.
  • A shortfall the operator is never told about.

What is expected of EVAgent

Every reported number comes from the CVXPY solve and passes six verification conditions before it reaches the operator. When they cannot pass, the day is escalated to a person rather than answered. The target for the solver-grounded row: solved + escalated = 100 %, wrong, unflagged = 0.

The rows of the table

Three blocks, and each row adds exactly one factor to the row above it. That is the whole design: if two rows differed by two things, no result could say which of the two mattered. The step that carries the paper's claim is ReAct to EVAgent, which differ only by the verification gate.

Apart from these sit two quantities computed on every day and printed next to the table, CVXPY optimum (optimum), Charge as soon as possible (charge_asap). Neither reads the request text, so neither is a method being compared: one is the optimum the cost gap is measured against, the other the uncontrolled rule the cost reduction is measured from.
